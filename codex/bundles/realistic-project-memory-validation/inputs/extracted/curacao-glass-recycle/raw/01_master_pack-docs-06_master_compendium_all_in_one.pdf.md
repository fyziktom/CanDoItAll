### Page 10
This document expands the initial concept note into a pre-FEED level business and technical plan. It is intentionally more conservative where real-world bottlenecks exist, especially around feedstock capture, usable waste heat, process water and island-specific execution risk. Why?  Solving trouble with glass waste on the ABC islands  Creating locally necessary material for building construction  Creating new jobs and support economy during factory building (preferred local suppliers) 1. Executive framing The project converts mixed waste glass from Curaçao (and ideal from Aruba and Bonaire too) into two core products: foam-glass aggregate and foam-glass blocks. The reference production concept is a 30 m electric tunnel kiln with annual output of about 19,162 m³/yr at 75% calendar utilization, equivalent to about 2,951 t/yr of finished product. The central strategic logic is import substitution and waste reduction. The plant does not need enormous tonnage to operate. In the base case it needs only about 3,451 t/yr of mixed raw glass at the factory gate. That is small enough to be plausible on Curaçao, but large enough that it still requires deliberate collection contracts rather than passive assumptions. Three corrections materially change the economics compared with an optimistic first-pass concept:  The kiln's recoverable low-grade heat should not be valued as if 150–250 kW can always be consumed. In the base case the realistic wash + dry thermal sink is only about 159 MWhth/yr on average, or roughly 73 kWth if the wash line runs one 8-hour shift.  Dedicated desalination is not justified for phase 1. Process make-up water is only about 248 m³/yr, so buying industrial water is cheaper and far simpler if a utility connection exists.  A battery should be justified primarily by resilience, power-quality support and operating flexibility, not by energy arbitrage. The current industrial high/low tariff spread is too small for a pure tariff-arbitrage case. 2. What the plant will make and why it should exist The plant will manufacture two foam-glass product families from mixed bottle and flat-glass waste:  Foam-glass aggregate: 11,498 m³/yr in the base case. This is the lower-finishing, easier-to-place product. It is relevant for lightweight fill, drainage layers, sub-base, roof build-ups and insulating fill.  Foam-glass blocks / bricks: 7,665 m³/yr in the base case. This product commands a higher selling price but requires stricter process control, cutting, trimming and pallet handling.

### Page 11
A dual-product strategy is important on an island. Aggregate gives a broad local outlet and tolerates a slightly wider process window. Blocks create margin and visibility, but they should not be the only route to market. The recommended starting mix is 60% aggregate and 40% blocks by volume, with the option to flex after market testing. The plant should not be treated as a generic recycling line. It is a specialty thermal-processing plant that happens to use waste glass as feedstock. That means the real value drivers are recipe stability, thermal control, capture logistics, and local market realization rather than the mere fact that a kiln exists. Figure 1. Detailed process flow used for the base concept. 3. Demand and market logic The local business case depends on replacing imported lightweight fill and insulation materials whose delivered island cost is elevated by freight, handling and inventory. That is why the plan deliberately uses local selling-price ranges rather than mainland commodity assumptions. In the base scenario the model uses 165 USD/m³ for aggregate and 240 USD/m³ for blocks. These are not 'guaranteed' market prices, with actual geopolitical situation you can expect large inflation in next years. They are planning values that still need confirmation by contractor interviews, pilot samples and delivered-import benchmarking. The right commercial posture is not 'cheap recycled material'. It is 'local engineered lightweight insulation / fill with lower logistics friction, shorter lead time and visible environmental value'.

The plant's base mixed-glass requirement is about 3,451 t/yr. This is the single most important physical constraint because the kiln prefers continuous operation and because feedstock shortfall is much harder to fix later than payroll or packaging cost. There is no reliable single official number in the project pack that proves, by itself, that Curaçao already delivers this exact quantity of clean and capturable mixed glass. Therefore the project should be framed honestly: feedstock sufficiency must be demonstrated by a capture model plus signed contracts, not by wishful top-down waste percentages. The most practical approach is a bottom-up capture model built from four streams:  Residents and retail drop-off.  Stayover tourism / HORECA (hotels, bars, restaurants, resorts).  Construction and demolition flat glass from windows and renovation.  Optional neighboring-island top-up only as a buffer, not the primary business case.

The practical implication is straightforward. If all hotel / bar material were delivered directly to the plant by small route trucks, the model implies roughly 986 loaded trips per year, or a little over three loaded routes per collection day. If glass is first consolidated into a central transfer point and then line-hauled to the plant in 7 t loads, that falls to roughly 493 loaded factory- inbound trips per year. Process and technology basis The chosen flow sheet is intentionally conventional rather than exotic. That improves serviceability and de-risks spare parts on an island:  Receiving, stockpiling and pre-sort.

### Page 16
 Mechanical separation: trommel / screens, ferrous and non-ferrous metal removal, optical sorting.  Wash, rinse, dryer and recirculating water loop with filter-press sludge handling.  Coarse crushing and fine milling to glass flour.  Silos, gravimetric dosing and intensive mixing with a foaming package and process aids.  Forming and continuous passage through a 30 m electric tunnel kiln.  Controlled cooling and split into two finishing streams: block trimming / palletizing and aggregate crushing / bagging. That package can include the foaming agent itself plus one or more process aids. The essential insight is that heat recovery must be matched to a real thermal sink. The initial concept note discussed much larger annual savings from 150–250 kW of recovered heat. That is not supported by the wash/dry duty actually required for this plant. In the base case, wash and dry together need only about 159 MWhth/yr. If the wash line ran in a single 8-hour daily shift, that equates to roughly 72.7 kWth average thermal demand during the shift. That is useful, but it is far below a continuously usable 150–250 kW recovery case. This changes design decisions immediately:  A modest heat-recovery loop is justified.  A small hot-water buffer is justified to decouple the kiln from wash scheduling.  A very large thermal store is not justified unless a second heat customer exists on site.  Projected savings must be capped by actual heat consumption, not by theoretical recoverable exhaust heat. A 20 m³ hot-water buffer at a 35°C working temperature spread stores about 0.81 MWhth and is adequate to shift more than one operating day of wash/dry demand. A 70 m³ store would be materially oversized unless another process or neighboring plant bought the heat. Figure 6. Energy, water and resilience schematic used for the base concept.

The same logic applies to seawater cooling. Electrical resilience, battery role and diesel backup The electrical system should be designed as an industrial-quality micro-system embedded in the wider grid rather than as a standalone islanded plant. The main reasons are kiln continuity, harmonic management, and fast response to grid disturbances. The 1 MW / 2 MWh battery gives roughly 3.6 h of autonomy against the kiln's modeled running average load, and about 6.7 h of autonomy against a 300 kW hot-hold essential-load case. That makes it valuable for ride-through, controlled load reduction and black-start support while the diesel generator starts or the grid recovers. The battery should therefore be justified on four grounds:  ride-through during brief grid events;  fast load shaping and possible participation in system balancing;  smoother self-consumption of PV generation;  support for a controlled rather than chaotic response to outages. What the battery should not be sold as is a classic time-of-use arbitrage machine. The tariff spread between industrial high and low periods is too small to justify that story on its own. Table 6. Site, buildings and brownfield reality Using a former refinery parcel is strategically attractive. They shift cost from greenfield civils into diligence, environmental management and uncertainty. The reference site plan assumes a planning area of roughly four hectares.
